## Safety

- **Never be under a car held up by a jack alone.** Use jack stands or a lift. Mazda's manual shows jacking positions for the factory jack only, and it does not publish stand or lift points in the text. ⚠️ We have not yet verified stand and lift points for this guide. If you use a lift, set its locks.
- **Shake the car before you trust it.** Give it a firm shove once it is up. If it moves more than you expect, lower it and reset.
- **Helpers stay clear while the car is moving up or down.** Nobody walks under it during lifting or lowering, and nobody goes under at all unless the adult in charge says so.
- **Let the exhaust cool.** The exhaust and the brakes stay hot long after a drive.
- **Wear eye protection.** Rust and road grit fall on your face when you look up at a car.
- **Do not wet the brakes.** If you hose the underside, Mazda warns that wet brakes are dangerous, and tells you to dry them by driving slowly and applying the brakes lightly several times before you rely on them.

## Why this exists

A car tells you about problems long before they become failures, but only in the places you do not normally look. Most of what goes wrong under a Miata is slow: a bushing that hardens and splits over years, a boot that cracks and lets the grease out, a seep that starts as a damp film, rust that starts in a seam and works outward. None of it shows from the driver's seat. All of it is easy to spot from underneath, and cheap to deal with at that stage.

The method is not a checklist. It is a habit of comparison, and it works in three ways at once. First, you compare the left side with the right. The car is close to symmetrical, so a part that looks different from its twin has probably changed. Second, you compare this year with last year, which is why a photograph from the same angle is worth so much. Third, you compare what you see with what you know healthy looks like, which is what the rest of this guide is for. If you do not know what normal is, the other side tells you. If something is different on both sides, it is probably normal for the car, and you note that too.

This is why Mazda's tables spread the inspection across a long list of items. The underbody list in the maintenance tables covers brake lines and hoses, steering linkages, suspension and ball joints, wheel bearing play, driveshaft boots, fuel lines and hoses, bolts and nuts on the chassis and body, and the exhaust and heat shields. A yearly pass over the whole car covers every one of them at once, and for a car that sits as much as it is driven, the calendar is the right trigger.

Road salt and chemicals make the whole job more important. Mazda says they collect on the underbody and speed up rusting of the fuel lines, frame, floor pan, and exhaust, even where those parts carry an anti-corrosion coating.

## What healthy looks like

- **Dry.** Underneath, a little dust and road film is normal. Wet, shiny, or dark trails are not. Fresh wetness looks glossy. Old seeps are crusted with dirt.
- **Rubber is supple and intact.** Boots, bushings, and hoses are smooth, with no cracks, splits, bulges, or torn edges.
- **Metal is solid.** Surface rust on unpainted fasteners and rotor faces is ordinary. Flaking scale, bubbling paint, or holes are not.
- **Left matches right.** Parts are in the same positions, at the same angles, with the same wear.
- **Fasteners look undisturbed.** Paint or torque marks, if there are any, line up. Nothing is missing or hanging.
- **Drain holes are open.** The lower edges of the doors, the rocker panels, and the frame members all have drain holes, and Mazda says trapped water there causes rust.

## Old versus new

For each wear item, learn what new looks like beside what needs work. A photo of the real worn part next to a new one teaches more than a paragraph, so we will add them as we get them. ⚠️ Photos of each pair belong here once we have them.

| Part | New or healthy | Worn out or needs work |
|---|---|---|
| Rubber bushings | Smooth, evenly seated in the metal sleeve, no splits | Cracked, split, bulging, squeezed out of the sleeve, or shifted off center |
| Rubber boots (steering, axle, ball joints) | Intact, clean, grease inside | Torn, cracked, or split, with grease flung around or a dry, dirty joint |
| Shock absorbers | Dry body, no film | Oily film running down the body, or oil dripping from the seal |
| Brake rotors | Smooth, even face | Deep grooves, a pronounced lip at the edge, heavy rust patches, or visible cracks |
| Brake pads | Plenty of friction material, even wear | Thin material, uneven wear, or a pad worn more on one side than the other. ⚠️ Mazda's minimum thickness is not in the owner's manual |
| Brake hoses and lines | Smooth, dry, secure on their clips | Cracked, bulging, wet, or rubbed, or a line with heavy rust |
| Tires | Even wear across the tread, readable date code | Wear on one edge, scalloped or feathered tread, bulges, cracks in the sidewall, or a date code that is more than 5 years old by our rule |
| Exhaust hangers and heat shields | Firm rubber hangers, secure shields | Stretched or cracked hangers, a rattling or loose shield, or fresh soot at a joint |
| Rust | Light surface rust on bare fasteners | Scale, bubbling paint, or a hole. Poke with a screwdriver handle only if you are sure it will not make things worse |

## The method

Pick an end, start there, and go to the other end. Front to back works, and so does back to front. What matters is that you use the same order every year, because a repeatable order is how you stop skipping things. Our first pass goes front to back.

Then we do it twice. After the first pass, we organize the notes, then walk the car again from back to front and take a second set. The two sets get compared side by side. Where they agree, you can trust the finding. Where one pass saw something the other missed, you have found a blind spot. Where the two passes disagree with each other, you have found something worth a third look, and often something worth a photograph. The second pass is also quicker, because you now know where to look.

For each area, look at one side, then the other, before you move on. Say out loud what is different. When something does not match, find the cause. If you cannot tell whether a difference matters, photograph it, note it, and look again at the next inspection.

### Front

- **Tires and wheels.** Look at the tread across the whole width, the sidewalls, the valve stems, and the date code. Look for uneven wear, which is a clue about alignment and suspension.
- **Brakes.** Look at each rotor and pad, the caliper, and the brake hose. Check for fluid wetness around the caliper and the hose ends.
- **Suspension.** Look closely. Check the control arms, the links, the bushings at each end, the ball joint boots, the springs, the shocks, and the sway bar and its links and bushings. Look for anything worn or anything that does not match its twin.
- **Steering.** Look at the rack and its boots, the tie rod ends and their boots, and the linkages. Mazda lists steering operation and linkages as an inspection item.
- **Engine underside.** Look at the oil pan and the drain plug for seeps, the oil filter, and the underbody cover. Look for drips, wetness, or anything hanging.

### Middle

- **Transmission and its mounts.** Look at the housing for seeps, the mounts for cracks, and the shifter area for wetness.
- **Driveshaft and its supports.** Look for movement, play, or wetness, and look at the boots Mazda lists as driveshaft dust boots.
- **Fuel lines and hoses.** Trace them where you can. Look for rubbing, cracks, wetness, or heavy rust on the lines.
- **Brake lines.** Trace the hard lines along the car. Look for rust, rubbing, or damage. These lines are the reason the underbody matters.
- **Floor pan, frame rails, and seams.** Look for flaking, bubbling, and holes. Check that the frame member drain holes are open.

### Rear

- **Differential.** Look at the housing and the seals for seeps, and at the mount for cracks.
- **Axles and boots.** Look at each boot and the joints for tears, grease, or looseness.
- **Rear suspension.** As in the front: arms, links, bushings, springs, shocks, sway bar, and the fasteners, left against right.
- **Rear brakes.** Rotors, pads, calipers, hoses, and the parking brake mechanism and cables.
- **Exhaust.** Follow it from the engine to the tip. Look at the hangers, the joints, the heat shields, and the tip. Rust, fresh soot, and rattles all point to a problem.
- **Fuel tank and its straps.** Look for rust, wetness, and loose or damaged straps.

### The body

- **Rocker panels and door bottoms.** Check that the drain holes in the lower edges of the doors and rocker panels are open, as Mazda says to. Check the lower edges and seams for bubbling or rust.
- **Wheel arches.** Look at the inner and outer lips for rust, damage, and packed mud.
- **Convertible top drains.** The drain filters are a separate job in the [convertible top guide](convertible-top-drains.md). Look at where the drains exit under the car, just inboard of the door sills.
- **Doors.** The door striker bushings and the hinges get a visual look once a year during this pass. They wear slowly from years of opening and closing. If a door is hard to close or latch, examine both. Panel gaps are an optional watch item for owners who notice them. Mazda's S2 table also lists lubricating all locks and hinges every 6 months.

## Steps

1. **Pick the day.** A dry day with the car cool, on a level surface, with time to look.
2. **Set up.** Put the car on the lift or on stands, and shake it. Set up good light. Have the camera ready.
3. **Take your baseline photos.** At each area, take a photo from the same angle every year, and one of anything unusual. Date them.
4. **Work your order.** Go from one end to the other, comparing left to right in each area.
5. **Note what you find.** For each item, note whether it is the same as last year, the same as its twin, or different. Write it down. When the first pass is done, organize the notes.
6. **Walk it again, back to front.** Take a second set of notes without looking at the first. Then compare the two sets. Anything one pass saw and the other missed, or where the two passes disagree, gets a closer look.
7. **Handle what is simple.** If a fastener is loose, clean it and tighten it to specification. Mazda says to tighten the bolts and nuts on the chassis and body at set intervals. ⚠️ Mazda does not list which bolts or their torque values in the owner's manual.
8. **Sort what is not simple.** Anything that needs repair goes on a list. Decide whether it is a today job, a this season job, or a watch item for next year.
9. **Look at the doors, hinges, and drains.** Finish with the body checks above.
10. **Look up recalls.** Look up your VIN on the NHTSA recall site while you are at it. It takes a minute and costs nothing.
11. **Lower the car.** Set it down carefully, take the stands out, and clear your tools.
12. **Log it.** Write down the date, the mileage, what you found, what you did, and what you are watching, and note where the two passes disagreed.

## When it goes wrong

- **You find a fresh wet spot.** Wipe it clean, drive a day or two, and look again. If it comes back, follow it upward to find the source. Brake fluid, oil, coolant, and transmission or differential oil each have a different look and smell.
- **A boot is torn.** Replace it soon, before dirt gets into the joint it protects. A torn boot is a cheap part. A ruined joint is not.
- **A bushing is cracked or shifted.** Note which one and look at the matching one on the other side. A worn bushing makes the car feel sloppy before it fails.
- **You see structural rust.** Rust that weakens a frame rail, a suspension mount, or a seam is not a cosmetic issue. Get it looked at before you drive hard.
- **A brake line or hose looks damaged.** Do not drive the car until it is replaced.
- **You are not sure.** Photograph it, note it, and compare it with the other side. If you still are not sure, ask someone more experienced. A second opinion on a doubtful part costs nothing.

## Log it

Write down the date, the mileage, and a short note for each area. Keep the photos in dated folders. After two or three years, your log is the most valuable tool you own, because it turns "that looks a bit worn" into "that has changed since 2025."
