## Safety

This job puts you under a car and in front of its brakes. Both deserve respect.

- **Never work under a car held up by a jack alone.** Use jack stands or a lift, on the points your owner's manual shows for lifting. ⚠️ We have not yet verified the exact lift and stand points for this guide.
- **Brake fluid eats paint.** Keep rags handy, wipe spills at once, and rinse with water.
- **Wear gloves and eye protection.** Fluid in the eye is a bad afternoon.
- **Never let the reservoir run dry while you bleed.** Air pulled into the master cylinder is a much bigger job to chase out than the one you set out to do.
- **Test the brakes before you drive anywhere.** A firm pedal in the driveway comes first. Then a slow stop in an empty lot. Then the road.
- **Used fluid is hazardous waste.** Take it to a parts store or waste facility that accepts it. Do not pour it down a drain or onto the ground.

## Why this exists

Brake fluid does one job, which is to carry the force from your foot to the calipers. It can do that because liquid does not compress. The catch is that the fluid is hygroscopic, meaning it pulls water out of the air, slowly, through the reservoir cap and through the rubber hoses themselves. The brake system is sealed, but it is not hermetic.

Water causes two problems. First, it lowers the boiling point of the fluid. Brakes make heat, and fluid that boils turns into gas, and gas compresses. That is the soft, sinking pedal people describe after a hard stop. Second, water corrodes the inside of calipers and the parts of the stability control unit, which are the expensive things to replace.

This is why the interval is a number of months and not a number of miles. The fluid absorbs moisture when the car sits, and if you drive a few days a week it sits a lot.

The clutch is part of the same story. According to ND owners, the clutch hydraulics draw from the same reservoir as the brakes (⚠️ confirm by looking at your own car). Same reservoir means the clutch circuit shares the same aging fluid. Mazda's published schedule gives a replacement interval for the brake fluid and only level checks for the clutch. Our practice is to flush the clutch whenever we flush the brakes, because the fluid is the same, the reservoir is the same, and owners report the slave cylinder is easy to reach from underneath. It adds little time and keeps the whole shared system fresh. That is our habit, and not a factory requirement.

## What healthy looks like

Learn this baseline first. Every other section depends on it.

- **Fluid color.** New fluid is clear to a light amber. As it ages it darkens toward brown. Look at it every time you open the hood and you will notice the drift long before it is a problem.
- **Fluid level.** Between the MIN and MAX marks on the reservoir. A level that drops over time as the pads wear is normal. A level that drops fast is not.
- **Brake pedal.** Firm, the same height every time, and no sinking when you hold steady pressure.
- **Clutch pedal.** Smooth, consistent travel, with the same engagement point every day.
- **Around the wheels.** Dry. No wet spots on the inside of the wheel, no dark trails below the calipers, bleeder dust caps in place.

## Steps

1. **Check what you have.** Look at the reservoir. Note the level and the color, because that is your before picture. Check that you have enough fresh fluid and that the bottle is newly opened, since opened fluid absorbs water.
2. **Draw down the old fluid.** Use a clean baster or syringe to remove most of the old fluid from the reservoir, then refill with fresh fluid to the MAX mark. This puts fresh fluid at the start of the system, so every bleed pushes out more old fluid and less of the new.
3. **Lift the car safely.** Raise it, set it on stands or the lift, and take the wheels off. Give the car a firm shove to confirm it is stable before you get under it.
4. **Choose a bleed order and stick to it.** Owners disagree. Some work from the wheel farthest from the master cylinder to the nearest. A former technician in the owner threads starts with the shortest run to flush the master cylinder end faster. Order does not matter when vacuum bleeding. One poster notes the right front line on the ND detours through the stability control unit, which makes "farthest" a fuzzy idea. Pick one order, write it down, and use it every time. A common starting point is right rear, left rear, right front, left front, then the clutch. ⚠️ Confirm the sequence against Mazda's service information.
5. **Bleed one corner.** Put the clear hose on the bleeder screw and the other end in the catch bottle, with the hose end under a little fluid so air cannot be drawn back in. Open the bleeder a small amount, have your helper press the pedal slowly and hold it, close the bleeder, then let the pedal return. Or use a one-way-valve hose, a vacuum bleeder, or a pressure bleeder to do it alone. Check the reservoir after every few strokes and top it up.
6. **Watch the fluid, not the count.** Keep going until the fluid in the hose looks like the fresh fluid in the bottle and no bubbles appear. How much that takes depends on how old the fluid is.
7. **Close up and move on.** Close the bleeder, wipe it, replace the dust cap, and move to the next corner in your order.
8. **Bleed the clutch.** The clutch slave cylinder has its own bleeder, and owners report it is reachable from underneath the car. Bleed it the same way as a brake corner. ⚠️ Its exact location is to be confirmed and photographed on the car. Keep the reservoir topped up while you do it.
9. **Set the level.** Fill the reservoir to the MAX mark and close the cap.
10. **Wheels on.** Refit the wheels, lower the car, and torque the lug nuts to the figure in your owner's manual. ⚠️ We have not yet put that figure into this guide's Verification block, so read it from your manual.
11. **Test.** With the car stopped, press the brake pedal. It should be firm and should not sink. Check the clutch pedal for normal feel and engagement. Do a slow stop in a safe, empty area before you rely on the brakes.
12. **Check again.** After the first drive, look under the car and at each wheel for leaks, and recheck the reservoir level.

## When it goes wrong

- **Spongy pedal after bleeding.** There is probably still air somewhere. Repeat the bleed, paying attention to any corner where you saw bubbles.
- **Pedal sinks slowly under steady pressure.** Stop and find the cause before driving. This can mean a leak or a failing master cylinder.
- **Clutch pedal sticks, or engagement feels different.** Air in the clutch circuit is the usual cause. Bleed the slave again.
- **Fluid on the floor or wheel.** Find it before the car moves. A weeping bleeder screw that is not tight is the cheap answer. A leaking caliper or hose is not.
- **Reservoir ran low or dry during the job.** Refill, then redo the bleed. Take extra care and read the manual section on air in the system before you drive.

## Log it

Write down the date, the mileage, the fluid brand and type, and your bleed order. Then set the next date 24 months out. Notes like these are how you spot drift: if the fluid came out darker than last time, you will know.
